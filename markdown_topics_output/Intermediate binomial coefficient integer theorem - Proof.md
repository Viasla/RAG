# Proof
The proof proceeds by induction on \(k\). For \(k=1\) the claim is trivial because \(\binom{n}{1}=n\) is an integer and the single multiplication step yields \(n\). Assume the claim holds for all \(k'<k\). Write \(\binom{n}{k}=\frac{n}{k}\cdot\binom{n-1}{k-1}\). By the inductive hypothesis, the computation of \(\binom{n-1}{k-1}\) using alternating multiplication and division produces only integers that never exceed \(\binom{n-1}{k-1}\). Since \(k\) divides \(n\) in the product \(\frac{n}{k}\) after the appropriate cancellations (the numerator contains a factor that is a multiple of \(k\)), the division step is exact, preserving integrality. Moreover, because \(\frac{n}{k}\le \frac{n}{1}=n\) and \(\binom{n-1}{k-1}\le\binom{n}{k}\), the product of these two integers cannot exceed \(\binom{n}{k}\). Hence every intermediate value in the full alternating sequence remains an integer and is bounded above by the final binomial coefficient.
