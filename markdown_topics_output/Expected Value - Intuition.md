# Intuition
The dice game illustrates the intuition behind expected value: by simulating many rolls and observing that the relative frequencies approach the theoretical probability $1/6$, one sees that the long‑run average gain approaches the weighted sum of outcomes, suggesting that the expected value is the average outcome one would anticipate over many repetitions.

The expected value provides a rational way to evaluate decisions when outcomes are uncertain, especially in situations where repeating the experiment many times is impractical, as illustrated by the betting example involving presidential death dates.

In decision theory, expected value acts as a criterion for choosing between strategies by comparing the weighted average of possible payoffs, exemplified by Pascal’s wager and the pricing of life annuities.

Expected value can be interpreted as the long‑run average gain or loss per play of a game, which underlies casino strategies such as Thorp’s blackjack system and the calculation of fair bets in roulette.

The expected value $E(X)$ represents the long‑run average result one would obtain by repeating the random experiment many times and averaging the observed values of $X$. It is the value that “should be expected” in the sense of a theoretical mean of the distribution.
