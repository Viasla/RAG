# Historical Notes
Simon Newcomb first observed the phenomenon in 1881, noting that the first pages of logarithm tables wore out faster because the first digit "1" occurs more often than other digits, and he published his observations in the American Journal of Mathematics.

T. P. Hill provided a modern general description of the Benford distribution in his 1995 paper "The Significant Digit Phenomenon," extending the idea to the first d digits of integers, though this exposition restricts attention to the first digit.

M. Nigrini popularized the use of Benford's law as a forensic tool in the early 2000s, advocating its application to detect irregularities in financial records, bookkeeping entries, checks, and tax returns.

An empirical illustration of the law’s fit to real data appears in the analysis of President Bill Clinton’s tax returns over a 13‑year period, where the observed leading‑digit frequencies (shown as circles) closely match the theoretical Benford values (shown as squares).
