De Moivre had independently derived the approximation without identifying the constant π, later discovering that the constant B appearing in the approximation of the binomial distribution equals √c, where c is the circumference of the unit circle (c=2π).

The central limit theorem was first proven by de Moivre using Stirling’s formula, connecting combinatorial probabilities to the normal distribution and laying the groundwork for later developments in probability theory.

De Moivre, in his *Doctrine of Chances*, introduced the first published proof of what is now called the Central Limit Theorem, using an approximation to factorials that later became known as Stirling's formula.  Although De Moivre had derived the factorial approximation before Stirling, he did not determine the constant ( sqrt{2pi} ), a fact he acknowledges but considers unnecessary for the main result.  The proof is found in De Moivre’s book and is later discussed in F. N. David’s *Games, Gods and Gambling* (London: Griffin, 1962).

The CLT was originally formulated for the binomial expansion ((a+b)^n) and for sums of independent trials.  De Moivre’s approach treats the sum of terms in the binomial series, providing practical rules to estimate the degree of assent given to experiments.  This early 18th‑century work laid the foundation for the modern theorem, which applies to any independent trial process with finite variance.

The Central Limit Theorem for discrete independent trials was first formalized in Theorem 9.4, which extends classical results for Bernoulli trials to arbitrary discrete distributions with common mean μ and variance σ².  The text notes that the assumption of identical distributions can be weakened; J. W. Lindeberg identified a condition on a sequence {X_n} that guarantees asymptotic normality of S_n, and W. Feller later proved that this Lindeberg condition is also necessary, implying that without it the sum will not converge to a normal distribution.  These developments are referenced to Feller’s 1968 *Introduction to Probability Theory and its Applications*, vol. 1, p. 254.

Theorem 9.5, presented in section 9.2 of the source text, is a form of the Central Limit Theorem for sequences of independent discrete random variables.  It is motivated by the long‑standing genetic question posed in the 1800s, particularly by Francis Galton’s study of the distribution of adult heights.  Galton observed that heights are strongly influenced by genetics yet the observed distribution is roughly normal, which seemed paradoxical because a single gene or a few parental effects would produce a multimodal distribution.  The multiple‑gene hypothesis, which assumes a large number of genes each contributing a small random effect, reconciles this by invoking the Central Limit Theorem to explain why the sum of many small independent genetic effects yields a normal distribution.  The text also refers to an exercise (Exercise 15) that emphasizes the necessity of the condition that the variances s_n^2 diverge to infinity for the theorem to hold.

Laplace earlier showed that the sum of many independent identically distributed random variables is approximately normal.

Quetelet empirically observed normal distribution in real data and gave a method for fitting the normal curve to a given data set.

Galton invented the quincunx (Galton board) and demonstrated that a binomial distribution becomes approximately normal for large values of the parameter n, physically illustrating the central limit theorem.

Galton’s quincunx experiments also showed that the convolution of two normal distributions yields another normal distribution, reinforcing the idea that sums of independent effects are normal.

Sweet pea seed experiments by Galton, which produced normally distributed offspring weights with the same variance as the parent groups, motivated the concept of regression to the mean; while not a counterexample, these studies provide historical context for the robustness of normality under the CLT.
