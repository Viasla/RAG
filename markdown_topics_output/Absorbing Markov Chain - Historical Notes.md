# Historical Notes
The notion of an absorbing Markov chain is introduced in Section 11.2 as a special class of Markov chains that are easier to study analytically than general chains. The text presents the concept alongside the earlier example of a stepping‑stone model, noting that although the model is easy to simulate, its analysis relies on absorbing‑chain theory.

The material presents absorbing Markov chains in the context of several textbook examples (e.g., Example 11.13, the drunkard’s walk, Example 11.1, the Land of Oz) and a series of exercises that ask the reader to verify that particular models are absorbing.

H. Gonshor (1987) applied random walks to population genetics, showing that the absorption probability equals the initial gene proportion (American Math Monthly 94, 668‑671).

E. Brown (private communication) introduced the three‑card game example used to illustrate an absorbing Markov chain.

F. Roberts (1976) presented the gambler’s ruin as an absorbing chain in *Discrete Mathematical Models* (Prentice Hall).

W. W. Leontief (1966) connected input‑output economics to absorbing Markov chains in *Input‑Output Economics* (Oxford University Press).

S‑Y. R. Li (1980) used absorbing chains to compute expected waiting times for patterns in repeated experiments (Annals of Probability 8, 1171‑1176).

Li used a theorem from the theory of martingales (fair games) to justify that the expected amount the casino takes in equals the expected amount it pays out for pattern‑based gambling games, establishing the connection between martingale theory and absorbing Markov chains.

Guibas and Odlyzko (1981) provided an elegant combinatorial method to compute the expected time to reach a pattern such as HTH, showing that the sum of tail probabilities equals the expected absorption time, a result that parallels the martingale argument used by Li.
