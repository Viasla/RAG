# Historical Notes
The early history of probability saw mathematicians grapple with the St. Petersburg paradox; G. Cramer proposed bounding the payoff by assuming a finite amount of money in the world, while Daniel Bernoulli introduced utility functions such as the square‑root or logarithm to assign finite value to the otherwise divergent expected payment.

Huygens, in the 17th century, calculated the expected value of games using techniques that anticipated the modern definition of expected value, providing a justification for its use even when the experiment cannot be repeated many times, such as the curious case of three presidents who died on July 4.

Pascal’s argument, often called Pascal’s wager, is an early use of expected value to argue that a rational person should believe in God; he compared the expected values of believing versus not believing using the payoff structure $p(-u)+(1-p)v$ versus $p\cdot0+(1-p)(-x)$ and assumed an infinite reward $v$ for belief.

John Graunt, using mortality data collected from London parishes beginning in 1603, implicitly applied expected value concepts when he assumed a constant survival probability of $5/8$ per decade to construct his mortality table, an effort later refined by Edmond Halley.

Edward Thorp’s 1962 book *Beat the Dealer* applied expected value to blackjack, showing that a player could adopt a strategy that yields a positive expected winning, thereby changing casino perceptions of beatability.

The concept of expected value is first introduced for discrete random variables in Section 6.1, where it is defined as a weighted sum of possible outcomes with their probabilities; later, in Section 6.3, the definition is extended to continuous random variables via integration over the density function.
