# Historical Notes
The term “martingale” is introduced in the text as a synonym for a fair game, i.e., a gambling situation in which the conditional expectation of a player’s future fortune equals the current fortune. The discussion follows the historical development of expected value, tracing back to Pascal’s and Huygens’ 17th‑century work on symmetric bets and the computation of fair stakes, which laid the conceptual groundwork for later formalizations such as the martingale property.

Pascal’s reasoning about dividing stakes in a partially played series and Huygens’ propositions on equal‑probability outcomes illustrate early intuitive uses of expected value that later became formalized as the martingale condition in probability theory.

In 1711, Abraham de Moivre presented an ingenious derivation of the probability of ruin in his book *De Mesura Sortis*, which later was recognized as an early example of a martingale argument.

De Moivre assigned nominal values to the players' counters (e.g., $q/p$, $(q/p)^2$, …) so that an unfair game became a fair game; this transformation is explicitly identified in the text as a martingale.
