# Examples
Example 4.1: Rolling a fair die, let F be {X=6} and E be {X>4}. After learning E, the probability of F becomes P(F|E)=1/2.

Example 4.2: In a population of 100,000 women, 89,835 live to age 60 (E) and 57,062 live to age 80 (F). The conditional probability of reaching 80 given survival to 60 is 57,062/89,835≈0.6352.

Example 4.3: Three candidates A, B, C with original probabilities P(A)=2/5, P(B)=2/5, P(C)=1/5. If A drops out, the conditional probabilities become P(B|A)=2/3 and P(C|A)=1/3, assuming voters redistribute proportionally.

Example 4.5: Two urns with different colour compositions. The conditional probability of drawing a black ball given urn I is chosen is P(B|I)=2/5. The inverse conditional probability of having chosen urn I given a black ball is P(I|B)=4/9.

Example 4.6 (Monty Hall): Although not fully worked out, the problem illustrates the need to recompute probabilities after new information (the host revealing a goat) is revealed, a classic conditional probability puzzle.
