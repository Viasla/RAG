# Properties
For any constant c, V(cX)=c^2V(X) (Theorem 6.7).

Adding a constant does not affect variance: V(X+c)=V(X).

Variance is not additive in general: V(X+Y)≠V(X)+V(Y) unless X and Y are independent.

If X and Y are independent, V(X+Y)=V(X)+V(Y) (Theorem 6.8).

By induction, the variance of the sum of n mutually independent variables equals the sum of their variances.

For i.i.d. variables with variance σ^2, V(S_n)=nσ^2 and V(A_n)=σ^2/n, where S_n is the sum and A_n the average (Theorem 6.9).

The alternative formula V(X)=E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2 provides a practical computational shortcut.

Theorem 6.14 gives two basic properties: for any constant \(c\), \(V(cX)=c^2 V(X)\) (scaling by \(c\) multiplies variance by \(c^2\)), and adding a constant does not change variance, \(V(X+c)=V(X)\).

Theorem 6.15 provides an algebraic shortcut: \(V(X)=E(X^2)-\mu^2\), where \(\mu=E(X)\).

Theorem 6.16 states that for independent random variables \(X\) and \(Y\), the variance of their sum is the sum of their variances, \(V(X+Y)=V(X)+V(Y)\).
