# Interpretation
Stirling’s asymptotic expression allows one to estimate $n!$ for large $n$ without computing the product directly.  It underlies many results in combinatorics and probability, such as approximations for binomial coefficients and the normal (Gaussian) distribution.  The identification of $\pi$ in the formula connects factorial growth to geometric constants.

The refined inequality shows that the error in the Stirling approximation is bounded by simple exponential factors, confirming that the leading term \(\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^{n}\) captures the essential growth of \(n!\).

In the birthday‑type problem (19), Stirling's Formula is used to replace factorials in \((n)_d\) with their asymptotic forms, allowing the derivation of a simpler expression that can be logarithmically expanded to solve for \(d\) in terms of \(n\).

The approximation $b(2n,.5,n)\sim 1/\sqrt{\pi n}$ expresses that the probability of obtaining exactly half heads in $2n$ fair‑coin tosses decreases proportionally to $1/\sqrt{n}$, a result derived from Stirling's formula.

Stirling’s Formula supplies the bridge from discrete factorial expressions to analytic asymptotics, enabling the determination of whether infinite series like  (sum u_{2n}^{(m)}) converge or diverge. In the two‑dimensional case, the asymptotic  (u_{2n}^{(2)}sim 1/(pi n)) leads to a harmonic‑type series that diverges, ensuring eventual return. In the three‑dimensional case, the derived bound  (u_{2n}^{(3)}lesssim c'/n^{3/2}) produces a convergent p‑series, yielding a return probability strictly less than one. Thus, Stirling’s approximation is the key analytic tool that translates combinatorial counts into probabilistic conclusions.
