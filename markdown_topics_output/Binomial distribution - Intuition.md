# Intuition
The most likely number of successes in a binomial experiment is approximately np; as the number of trials n increases, the distribution becomes more concentrated around its mean np, and the peak probability decreases.

For p=1/2 the binomial distribution is symmetric about the center value, and for p=0.3 it is nearly symmetric, as seen in the plotted graphs.

The binomial distribution counts how many successes occur in a fixed number $n$ of independent trials when the probability of success in each trial is a constant $x$. It can be imagined as tossing a biased coin $n$ times and counting the heads. The distribution is discrete and only depends on two parameters: the number of trials and the success probability. When the success probability is unknown and treated as a random variable, the binomial distribution is used as the likelihood in a Bayesian updating procedure.

The binomial distribution models the number of successes in a fixed number of independent Bernoulli trials, where each trial has the same probability of success.

For a coin tossed n times with probability p of heads, the binomial random variable counts the number of heads that occur.

The binomial distribution describes the probability of a fixed number of successes in a fixed number of independent Bernoulli trials with probability p of success.

When the number of trials n is large and the success probability p is small, the binomial distribution can be approximated by a Poisson distribution with parameter λ= np.

In these problems the binomial distribution represents the number of successful outcomes in a fixed number of independent trials, each with a constant success probability.  It models situations where each trial is either a success or failure, such as a nuclear plant having a significant accident, a passenger showing up for a flight, or a randomly chosen coin being counterfeit.  The key intuition is that the total number of successes is governed by the number of trials and the probability of success on each trial.
