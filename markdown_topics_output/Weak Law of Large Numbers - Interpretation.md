# Interpretation
The weak law formalizes the frequency interpretation of probability: the long‑run relative frequency of an event (e.g., heads in coin tossing, successes in Bernoulli trials) converges in probability to the theoretical probability \(p\).

It shows that even when individual outcomes are unpredictable, averaging many independent repetitions yields a quantity that can be predicted with arbitrarily high confidence.

The weak law says that the sample average converges to the true mean *in probability*; that is, for any fixed tolerance the chance of a large deviation becomes negligible as the number of observations grows.

Unlike the strong law, the weak law does not guarantee that the sequence of averages will almost surely settle down to the limit; it only controls the probability of deviation at each finite stage.

In practical terms, the weak law justifies using the empirical average as an estimator of the mean when the sample size is large, because the estimator is highly likely to be close to the true value.
