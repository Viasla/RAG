# Properties
The sum Σ_{ω∈Ω}m(ω) must be convergent; without convergence the function cannot be a probability distribution.

The total sum equals exactly 1, guaranteeing that the distribution accounts for all possible outcomes.

Theorems 1.1, 1.2, and 1.4 (which hold for finite spaces) remain valid for countably infinite spaces under this definition.

A uniform distribution function, which assigns equal probability to each outcome, cannot be defined on a countably infinite sample space because the required sum would diverge.

Non‑negativity: m(ω)≥0 for all ω.

Normalization: Σ_{ω∈Ω}m(ω)=1.

Uniformity is possible only on finite sample spaces; on a countably infinite Ω a constant a with 0≤a≤1 cannot satisfy the normalization condition.

The function defined in (23) m(j)=(1−r)^j r is a valid distribution because it forms a geometric series summing to 1.

When a density $f$ exists, $F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt$, so $F$ is obtained by integrating the density over $(-\infty,x]$.

Differentiating $F$ yields the density: $\frac{d}{dx}F(x)=f(x)$.

In Example 2.13 the distribution function $F_X$ is continuous, even though the associated density $f_X$ is not, illustrating that continuity of $F$ does not imply continuity of $f$.
