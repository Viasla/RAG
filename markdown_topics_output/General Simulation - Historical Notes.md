# Historical Notes
The practice of carrying out repeated trials of a chance game has effectively been simulation since ancient times, with gamblers’ experiences prompting early probability problems.

Buffon tossed a coin 4040 times, noting 2048 heads and 1992 tails, to empirically study probability.

Weldon recorded 26,306 throws of 12 dice and Wolf recorded 100,000 single die throws without a computer, revealing potential biases in dice and roulette wheels.

Karl Pearson’s analysis of roulette tables led to the chi‑squared test for goodness of fit and highlighted the need for accurate random number generation.

In 1927 Tippett published a list of 41,600 digits selected from census reports to serve as a source of randomness.

The RAND Corporation printed a million‑number table generated from electronic noise in 1955, advancing random number tables.

Von Neumann introduced a squaring method (starting with a seed, squaring, and extracting middle digits) and later modular arithmetic sequences X_{n+1}=(aX_n+c) mod m for generating pseudo‑random numbers.

The Los Alamos scientists used Monte Carlo simulation to estimate neutron penetration in materials, a calculation beyond analytic reach.

Von Neumann and Ulam coined the term “Monte Carlo” for this computational simulation of random experiments.

Feller used computer simulations to illustrate probability concepts, such as fluctuations in coin tossing, and presented results from 10,000 simulated trials.
