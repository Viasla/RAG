# Historical Notes
In probability theory, the concept of independence between two events was first formalized in the context of discrete sample spaces, where events are subsets of a finite set of outcomes.  The same idea was later extended to continuous sample spaces, where events are measurable subsets of a continuous space.  In both cases, independence is defined through the equality of conditional and unconditional probabilities.  This extension allows one to apply the same intuitive notion—that knowing one event occurred does not alter the likelihood of the other—to continuous random experiments such as the spinner, dart game, and radioactive decay models discussed earlier in the text.

De Moivre defined independence and dependence in the 18th‑century text *The Doctrine of Chances*.  He described two events as independent when they have no connexion one with the other, and the happening of one neither forwards nor obstructs the happening of the other; in contrast he described dependent events as those for which the probability of one is altered by the happening of the other.

Earlier, the text introduced a Bernoulli trials process in which the random variables (X_{1},X_{2},ldots ,X_{n}) are called an independent trials process, indicating that successive trials are independent.  This exemplifies the concept of independence in a concrete probability model.
